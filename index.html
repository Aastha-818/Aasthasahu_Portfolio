01 / 06 · StepGenie

StepGeniea study app that stayed one product across 40+ screens.

A dark-theme medical exam-prep app used by 10,000+ people, held together by a design system I built before there were enough screens to justify one.

Type
Exam-prep app · web + mobile
Role
Product designer — owned UX, UI, design system
Year
2024
Team
Founder + N developers
Status
Live
Your image · StepGenie hero

The short version

Problem, decision, result

Problem

Exam prep is long sessions across a lot of content types — questions, explanations, progress. Every screen wanted to look different.

Decided

Build the system first: colour, type, component patterns. Then design the 40+ screens on top of it, so features could be added without redesigning.

Result

Shipped, in use by 10,000+ people. One real signal — retention, session length, a user quote, or “no metric available yet.”

Context

Who it’s for

Students preparing for a medical entrance exam. Which exam, and what the study loop looks like: practice questions? mock tests? spaced revision?

The product had to work as a website and on a phone, because the same person switches between a laptop at a desk and a phone in bed.

Problem

What was broken

What existed before you — an earlier version, a competitor students were using, or nothing? What did the founder ask for, and what was the real problem underneath the ask?

I researched the competitors students already had open in other tabs — name two — and what they got right that we couldn’t afford to get wrong.

Decisions

The forks

Considered

A light default, with dark mode as a setting — the safe choice for a “serious” education product.

Chose

Dark as the only theme, designed from the ground up rather than inverted.

Because the real reason — long night sessions? eye strain? brand? founder preference you pushed back on or agreed with?
Considered

Design screens first, extract a system later once patterns emerged.

Chose

A colour system, type scale and component patterns before the tenth screen existed.

Because the roadmap was long and the team was small. Every new feature after that was assembled, not designed from zero — which is how 40+ screens stayed one product.

A third fork specific to the study experience — how questions are answered, how progress is shown, how a wrong answer feels.

Design

What I made

Margin note. Pick eight screens, not forty. Each one should show a different job: onboarding, a question, a result, progress, an empty state, an error, settings, and one you’re proud of.

Result

What happened

Live, used by 10,000+ people. Anything measurable you’re allowed to share. If nothing, say “I don’t have access to post-launch numbers” — that reads as honest, not weak.

Reflection

What I’d do differently

One honest sentence. The thing you’d change if you started again tomorrow.

The short version

Problem, decision, result

Problem

A lab instrument needs an interface that runs on modest hardware, on a fixed screen, for people who use it all day and never read a manual.

Decided

Design for the workflow and the failure states first; make the interface a set of clear, information-dense screens rather than a “nice app.”

Result

Delivered to the hardware team / shipped on N devices / in use at N labs — whatever is true.

Context

Not a phone, not a browser

Bio Spectronics builds a biochemistry analyzer — an instrument that runs samples and reports results — powered by a Raspberry Pi with a display attached. I designed what appears on that display.

The hardware facts a designer needs: screen size and resolution, touch or buttons, viewing distance, refresh/latency limits, what the Pi could and couldn’t render.

Problem

What had to be true

This is the project I point to when someone asks whether I can design for complex, high-stakes, information-heavy systems. The constraints were the brief:

The forks

Considered

e.g. A consumer-style UI with cards, gestures and hidden menus.

Chose

e.g. Large, fixed, always-visible controls; results in tabular type; one action per screen.

Because the reason — gloves, glance-reading, training cost, error consequences.

NMIMS Centre for International Studiesthe front door of a dual-degree programme.

A redesigned homepage for NMIMS’s international dual-degree programme, built around the questions a prospective student asks first — and audited line by line against the client’s requirements before presenting.

Type
University website · homepage redesign
Role
Designer — direction, IA, UX, UI, responsive
Year
2025
Via
Kwebmaker Digital (agency) — confirm you can credit
Scope
Homepage
Status

Your image · NMIMS CIS hero

The short version

Problem, decision, result

Problem

A programme page carrying a lot of institutional content, where the person who matters — a student deciding whether to apply — had to hunt for the basics: which degrees, which partner universities, what it costs, how to apply.

Decided

Reorganise the homepage around the prospective-student journey, explore light and dark directions, and check every section against the written requirements before the client saw it.

Result

Homepage direction and design delivered and presented. Scope was the homepage; inner pages were not part of the final engagement.

Context

Who’s reading

A student — often with a parent beside them — comparing an international dual-degree against other options. Credibility (partner universities, accreditation) and clarity (what, where, how much) matter more than anything decorative.

Decisions

The forks

Considered

Keep the institution’s existing structure and restyle it.

Chose

Re-sequence the homepage around the student’s questions, then fit the institutional content into that order.

Because a homepage that answers “which degrees, with whom, how do I apply” in the first two screens does more for enrolment than any amount of polish on the old order.
Considered

One direction, presented as final.

Chose

Two directions — light and dark — built as real responsive pages, with a compliance audit against the requirements document.

Because a client comparing two real options makes faster, better decisions than one reacting to a single mockup — and the audit meant the review was about direction, not missing items.

Design

What I made

Reflection

Looking back

Margin note. Scope changes happen. Saying “homepage only” plainly reads as professional; hiding it reads as something else.
